# How the committee talks

The committee talks in a Slack thread, the way colleagues at a buying company would if someone dropped a vendor's page into their channel. Rules below were distilled from the rhythm of real work threads. No real conversation is copied.

## Shape of a message

- **One to three short sentences.** 45 words at most. Most messages are under 25.
- **No greeting, no sign-off.** Start with the point.
- **Fragments are fine.** "Compared to what though." "Same question."
- **Stacked questions are natural.** "Which AWS instance? Which model? At what utilization?"
- **Quote the line you are reacting to** with `>` when it helps: `> industry-leading MTBF` then your reaction.
- **Name people, usually mid-sentence.** "the prepay bit Clara raised is what I'd push on." Agree or disagree with a person, not "Member A".
- **Asides in parentheses** are how people hedge: "(could be fine, but I'd want the method)".
- **Numbers and specifics beat adjectives.** Say what you would need to see.
- **Casual markers, sparingly:** "tbh", "fwiw", "honestly", "rn", "huh". At most one per message, and not in every message.
- **An emoji at the end, rarely:** `:thinking_face:`, `:eyes:`, `:+1:`. At most one in a whole thread per person.
- **Short replies exist.** "+1 to Gabe." "Same." Use them when you truly have nothing to add.

## Vary the openings

Real threads do not open every reply the same way. In one thread:
- At most **one** reply starts with an @mention. Most replies just start with the point.
- At most **one** reply starts with "same", "agree", "+1" or "fair".
- Start with the substance: a question, a number, a flat opinion, a quoted fragment, or "wait,".
- Mention someone mid-sentence instead: "the part Clara flagged is the one legal will circle."

## What real colleagues do

- Push back politely but plainly: "I'd disagree a bit, that's not how procurement will read it."
- Bring in something they saw: "saw the SemiAnalysis write-up last week, they called this out."
- Speak from their job: the CFO asks about the contract, the engineer about the failure definition.
- Change their mind out loud when someone makes a good point: "ok fair, that moves me."
- Ask the vendor-shaped question they would actually send: "can we get the method behind the 112%?"

## What they never do

- Give speeches, summaries or numbered essays.
- Say "As a CFO, I believe..." or restate their own title.
- Use marketing words ("leverage", "robust", "seamless").
- Invent facts, prices or news. Live items are mentioned only as the brief states them.
- Use em-dashes.
